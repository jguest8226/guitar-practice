Guide: how this program works

Built from my Practice Journal. Covers the keys of C, G, D and A and the chords worship songs use most: the I, ii, IV, V and vi.

How to use this

  • One day = one small chunk, about 20 minutes. Do the day, tick its Done box (the date and time are logged automatically in Practice history; add your BPM and a quick note), and move to the next day tomorrow.
  • 4-week cycle, one key per week: C → G → D → A. Each week adds one sharp, so you're also walking the circle of fifths. After week 4, start over and work faster or add the Level 2 items.
  • Say it out loud. For every note you play, say its name and its job in the chord: "C, root… E, third… G, fifth." That's how the notes and degrees stick.
  • Metronome: start at 60 bpm with one note per click. When a drill is clean twice in a row, go up 5 bpm.
  • Every chart and fret number is on the Program page, organized week by week.

One-note moves (sus, 7ths and the "hidden" chords)

Every chord on this page starts as a 3-note triad. You get the rest by moving one note:

Want Move this note Major triad Minor triad
sus2 3rd down to the 2 down 2 frets down 1 fret
sus4 3rd up to the 4 up 1 fret up 2 frets
maj7 (I, IV) root down to the 7 down 1 fret —
7 / m7 (V, ii, vi) root down to the b7 down 2 frets down 2 frets

That last pair is the note in your journal: "Major triad: move the root down a whole step for dominant 7, a half step for major 7."

Hidden chords (these help you see the neck as one big map):

  • Imaj7 with no root is the iii triad. In C: Cmaj7 = C E G B, and E G B = Em.
  • IVmaj7 contains the vi triad. In C: Fmaj7 = F A C E, and A C E = Am.
  • vi m7 is the I triad over a vi bass. In C: Am7 = A C E G, which is C over A. That's why vi and I swap so easily in worship songs.
  • A 1st inversion is a slash chord. With the 3rd on the bottom, C (E G C) sounds like C/E. Same with G/B and D/F#, the classic worship bass walk-ups.

Voice leading (how to move between shapes)

Move as few fingers as possible. In C on the top strings:

  • C → F: x-x-x-5-5-3 → x-x-x-5-6-5. Two notes move one or two frets, and the C on the G string stays put.
  • C → G: x-x-x-5-5-3 → x-x-x-4-3-3.
  • C → Am: x-x-x-5-5-3 → x-x-x-5-5-5. Only one note moves (G up to A).

Look for this in every key: the closest shape of the next chord is almost always within 2 frets.

Notes on the fretboard

Sharps are shown, and each one is also a flat (C# = Db). Frets 12 and up repeat from fret 0. Bold dots = natural notes, grey = sharps/flats.

Quick landmarks: E–F and B–C have no sharp between them. Fret 5 on each string equals the next string open, except on the G string, where fret 4 equals the open B.

How to read the chord charts

  • Each chart is a chord box: strings run low E (left) to high e (right), and frets run top to bottom. × = don't play that string. A thick top line is the nut. Otherwise the number on the left (like 5fr) is the fret the top row starts on.
  • The dot labels tell you each note's job: R = root (orange), 3 and 5 = 3rd and 5th (black), 2 / 4 = the sus note (purple), 7 / b7 = the 7th (blue).
  • In the triad grids, the 3 shapes of each chord go up the neck from left to right. Under each chart: the inversion, the CAGED shape it comes from (to connect with your journal's diagrams), and the notes from low to high.
  • In the sus tables, each row shows the triad, its sus2 and its sus4 side by side, so you can see the one finger that moves.
  • Anything above fret 12 also works 12 frets lower when the open strings allow it.
